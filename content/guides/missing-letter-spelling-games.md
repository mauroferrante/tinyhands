# Missing-letter games and early spelling: why C_T beats copying words

Somewhere between four and seven, a child goes from knowing that letters exist to knowing that letters *spell*. It is one of the biggest leaps in early learning, and one of the easiest to make miserable. Worksheets of words to copy teach handwriting; they do not teach much about how words work. A missing letter does.

## The steps to spelling

Spelling is not one skill. It stacks:

1. **Letter recognition.** Knowing that this shape is a B.
2. **Letter sounds.** Knowing B says /b/. This is phonics, and it is the hinge of the whole thing.
3. **Hearing sounds in words.** Noticing that "cat" starts with /k/, has /a/ in the middle, ends with /t/. Teachers call this phonemic awareness.
4. **Mapping sounds to letters.** Writing C-A-T for the sounds you heard.

Most children arrive at four with step one and some of step two. Steps three and four are where school starts, and where a game can genuinely help.

## Why the missing letter works

Show a child a picture of a cat and the word C _ T, and something specific happens in their head. They say "cat" to themselves. They hear the middle sound. They hunt for the letter that makes it. That is steps three and four, exercised in three seconds, with a picture to anchor the meaning and the other letters as scaffolding.

Compare copying the word CAT off a card: the child looks, draws, and never has to hear a sound at all. Copying is fine for handwriting practice. It does not build spelling.

The missing-letter format also lets a game be gentle. There is one blank, so there is one decision, and the picture makes the word obvious. The child is never staring at an empty line.

## How Spell It Out plays

[Spell It Out](/games/spell-it-out/) shows an emoji, its word with one letter missing, and waits. On a laptop the child types the letter; on a tablet an on-screen keyboard appears and used letters gray out, so the choice narrows if they guess. A right answer fills the tile with a chime and sparkles. A wrong one shows the correct letter, costs one of three lives, and moves on. Streaks earn bigger celebrations, and the game remembers a best score.

Words start at three letters (CAT, DOG, SUN, BUS) and get longer. The missing letter moves around, so sometimes it is the first sound, sometimes the middle vowel, sometimes the last consonant, which quietly covers all three positions.

## Playing it without making it a test

Four-year-olds can smell a test. A few ways to keep it a game:

- **Say the word slowly, together.** "Caaat. What's in the middle?" Stretching the word is the single most useful thing a parent can do for early spelling.
- **Let them guess.** A wrong guess shows the right letter and moves on. That is a lesson, not a failure.
- **Cheer the streak, not the score.** Five in a row gets sparkles; make a fuss about that.
- **Stop at the lives.** When three lives are gone, the round ends with a friendly card. That is a fine place to stop for the day.
- **Cover the picture** for a confident six-year-old and read the word aloud instead. Now they are spelling from sound alone.

## On spellings

The word list avoids words that differ between American and British English (color/colour, gray/grey), so a child on either side of the Atlantic is never marked wrong for a regional difference. Common nouns with regular spelling were chosen deliberately; irregular words are a different lesson.

## From here

A child who is comfortable filling in a missing letter is ready to write whole short words from sound. Have them label their drawings. Spelling will be shaky and that is exactly right: "KAT" is a child who has heard every sound and is one rule away from correct.
