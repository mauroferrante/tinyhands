Teaching Toddlers Melodies: Do-Re-Mi, Ear Training and a Piano With Eight Keys

By Mauro Ferrante · Updated 2026-09-07 · 3 min read

Children sing before they talk in sentences and dance before they walk well. Music is not something we add to a child; it is something they arrive with. The question for parents is how to feed it, and the answer, for the first several years, has almost nothing to do with reading notes.

Ear first, always

Every music teacher will tell you the same thing: children who learn to hear music before they learn to read it become better musicians, and happier ones. Hearing means recognizing that a note is higher or lower than the last one, that a tune goes up and then comes back down, that a rhythm is steady or bumpy. Reading comes later, the same way reading words comes after speaking them.

So the first musical instrument for a small child should do three things: make a clear, pleasant sound; have few enough notes that the child can find their way around; and let them play real tunes quickly enough to feel like a musician.

Why eight keys

A full piano has 88 keys and is bewildering at three. Eight keys, one octave from Do to high Do, is enough for hundreds of children's songs. Twinkle Twinkle, Mary Had a Little Lamb, Hot Cross Buns, Happy Birthday and the theme from Ode to Joy all fit inside a single octave. Eight is also a number a small child can hold in their head, especially when each key has its own color.

Melody Maker is an eight-key piano built on that idea. The keys are big, colorful and labeled with their solfège names (Do, Re, Mi…), and on a laptop the letters A to K play them, which most children find before you point it out.

Three ways in

Free Play is just the piano. Tap, hear a note, tap another. Start here and stay here for a while; a three-year-old needs the instrument to feel like a toy before it feels like a lesson.

Learn Melodies is where the ear training happens. A friendly lion plays a short tune while a bouncing ball hops from key to key showing what he pressed. Then it is the child's turn to play it back. Get a note wrong and the lion plays the tune again, a little slower. Thirty melodies build in difficulty:

  • Levels 1 to 5 are three or four notes with a clear shape: up, down, a jump.
  • Levels 6 to 15 add held notes, rests and longer patterns.
  • Levels 16 onward are real songs, from Hot Cross Buns through Old MacDonald and Jingle Bells to Happy Birthday and Ode to Joy.

A star marks each completed melody and the next one is highlighted, so progress is visible.

Song Parade is play-along: notes fall toward the keyboard and a whale conducts. It is less about memory and more about timing, and older children love it.

What they are actually learning

  • Pitch discrimination. Higher and lower, the foundation of every musical skill.
  • Sequence memory. Holding four, then six, then ten notes in order. This is working memory with sound, and it transfers.
  • Rhythm. Melody Maker rates each note played back as steady, a bit off or very off, gently, with colored dots.
  • Confidence. Playing a tune everyone recognizes, at four, is a genuine thrill.

Tips from a parent

  1. Slow it down. There is a turtle button that slows the lion's demonstration. Use it. Four-year-olds need it on anything over six notes.
  2. Sing the note names. Do, Re, Mi out loud while they play. It anchors the pitch to a word.
  3. Landscape on phones. The keyboard needs the width.
  4. Don't correct, replay. The replay button plays the demo again. Let the game do the correcting; you do the encouraging.
  5. Stop while it's fun. One melody a day is a fine pace.

From here

Children who get through the melodies often want a real instrument. A cheap keyboard with full-size keys is the natural next step, and they will arrive already knowing what Do to Do sounds like.
