Following Directions and Map Play: What Kids Learn Exploring a Tiny Town

By Mauro Ferrante · Updated 2026-09-07 · 3 min read

"Go to your room, get your shoes, and bring them to the front door." Three steps. Watch a five-year-old set off confidently and come back with a toy dinosaur. This is not disobedience. It is working memory and route planning being asked to do more than they can yet manage, and both are skills that grow with practice.

Two skills hiding in one errand

Holding an instruction. A multi-step instruction has to be stored, kept in order, and checked off as you go. Most four-year-olds can hold two steps; three is a stretch; four is a lot until six or seven. When a child "forgets" step two, they often have not forgotten it so much as had it pushed out by whatever they saw on the way.

Knowing where things are. Children build mental maps of their world surprisingly early, but they are patchy: home, nursery, grandma's house, the park, connected by "the car goes for a while." Filling in the map, knowing that the shop is past the park and before the bridge, is a skill that comes from moving through a space repeatedly and paying attention.

Errands practice both at once, which is why they are hard, and why they are worth setting.

Practicing on a screen

A small, consistent world that a child can walk around again and again is good practice for the second skill, and errands inside it are good practice for the first. That is the whole design of Tiny Town.

It is Festival of Kindness day in an emoji village. Townsfolk have gifts for one another that need delivering. The child picks a character and walks: past the bakery, along the beach with its boats and dolphins, over to the airport where planes take off. Talk to a friend and collect their gift. Find the person it is for. Deliver it. Repeat until the festival is complete.

What is deliberately not in it: enemies, a timer, a score, any way to fail. Nothing chases you. The only surprise is a puppy who may decide to follow. A child can stop and watch the dolphins for as long as they like.

What it exercises

  • Holding a two-part instruction. "The baker has a gift for the pilot." Who, and where, kept in mind across the walk.
  • Building a mental map. After two or three visits, children stop wandering and start heading straight for the beach because they know where it is. That moment is the learning.
  • Reading short dialogue. The townsfolk speak in a sentence or two. Four-year-olds will need it read to them; six-year-olds read it themselves.
  • Planning. Older children notice they can carry two gifts and deliver both in one trip, which is genuine route optimization.
  • Kindness as the point. The goal is doing something nice for someone. It is a small thing in a game, and children notice.

Controls

On a laptop, the arrow keys or WASD walk the character, and walking into a person starts a conversation. On a tablet, tap where you want to go and the character walks there along the roads; tap a person to talk. Either way the world is calm enough that a child can learn the controls without pressure.

Playing it together

  • Ask before they set off. "Where do you think the pilot lives?" Predicting the route is the map skill made explicit.
  • Let them get lost. Being lost in a safe world with no timer is how the map fills in. Resist directing.
  • Retell the errand. "Who gave you the gift? Who is it for?" That is the instruction-holding skill, rehearsed out loud.
  • Notice the town. The planes take off on a schedule. The boats drift. Point things out; noticing landmarks is how real-world navigation works too.

From here

Children who enjoy Tiny Town's errands tend to enjoy real-world versions: a shopping list with three items they are in charge of, or drawing a map of the walk to school. The screen version is a rehearsal, and the rehearsal is safe.
