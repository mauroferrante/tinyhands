Why Toddlers Love Smashing the Keyboard (and a Free Game That Makes It Safe)

By Mauro Ferrante · Updated 2026-09-07 · 4 min read

If you have a laptop and a one-year-old, you already know the scene. You sit down to work, and a small person arrives at your elbow with a single mission: press every key, as fast as possible, while looking at your face for the reaction. Windows close. Emails send themselves. Somehow the display is upside down.

This guide is about why that happens, why it is worth encouraging rather than fighting, and how to give it a safe outlet with a keyboard smash game.

Why the keyboard is irresistible

A keyboard is a perfect toy for a toddler's stage of development, which is roughly "I do a thing, and the world does a thing back." Every key gives a click under the finger, and on a real computer every key does something visible. That is cause and effect at its purest, and between about ten months and three years it is the most interesting idea in the universe.

It also happens to be your keyboard. Toddlers copy what the adults they love spend time on. If you type all day, the keyboard is the most important object in the house, and getting their hands on it is a way of joining in.

What they actually learn from it

More than you would think, if the keyboard is allowed to respond.

  • Cause and effect. The core lesson. "My finger did that." It is the foundation for every later idea about how the world works.
  • Fine motor control. Pressing one key instead of a fistful takes real skill. Watch a two-year-old go from palms to a single pointing finger over a few weeks.
  • Turn-taking and attention. A game that responds to every press holds attention far longer than one that plays on its own.
  • Letters and numbers, eventually. Not at one. But by two and a half, the letters on the keys start to mean something, and a game that shows the letter when it is pressed makes that connection for them.

Making it safe

The problem with a real keyboard is that it is connected to a real computer. The fix is a game that takes over every key while it is open, so pressing Escape, Enter or the Windows key does nothing except pop another emoji.

That is exactly what Kaboom Keys does. Every key press pops a big colorful emoji with a sound. Letters, numbers, Space, the arrows, even Shift and Caps Lock each have their own. Grown-up shortcuts like Cmd+R still work, so you can reload or switch windows, but nothing a child can reach has an effect outside the game. On a tablet, tapping the screen does the same thing, and several fingers make several emojis.

A few practical tips:

  1. Open the game first, then sit the child at the keyboard. Starting from the desktop invites a detour.
  2. Use full screen. Kaboom Keys goes full screen on laptops so the browser bar is out of reach.
  3. Turn the volume to a level you can live with. There will be a lot of pops.
  4. If a key does nothing, it is probably Fn or a media key. Everything else responds.

ABC mode: from smashing to letters

Two parents asked for the same thing in the feedback form: "an ABC one, not random things." So Kaboom Keys has an ABC toggle in the corner. When it is on, letter and number keys show a giant letter card with a matching picture, A with an apple, B with a bee, Z with a zebra, and the other keys are ignored. On a tablet with no keyboard, each tap walks through the alphabet in order.

It is the same game, one switch away, and it grows with the child. One-year-olds smash; two-year-olds start to notice the letters; three-year-olds hunt for the letter of their name.

Looking for Tiny Fingers?

A lot of parents find this site while searching for Tiny Fingers, another keyboard smash site that has been around for years. If that is you: the idea is the same, and both are free. Kaboom Keys is one game inside Tiny Hands Play, a collection of eleven, so when your child outgrows smashing there is a stacking game, a memory game and a piano waiting on the same page. It has no ads, works with touch as well as keys, and has the ABC mode described above. Try both and keep whichever your child comes back to.

When to move on

Kaboom Keys is for the pure cause-and-effect stage. When your child starts wanting a goal, something to aim at, something to finish, Ball Bonanza gives them a ball to kick at animal friends, and Dress-Up Party lets them make choices. Both are still one-tap games with no way to lose.
